THE QUESTION

Is everyone else really more popular?

In Week 1, Spider-Man dominated incoming links. This week we asked a different question: what does this uneven network look like from an ordinary character’s neighborhood?

Our experiment chooses one of the 286 nonisolated characters uniformly, then one of that character’s neighbors uniformly. We count undirected connections for both. The expected person degree is 10.03; the expected friend degree is 24.22. In 75.3% of pairs, the friend has at least as many connections, with ties included.

These are exact averages over all possible draws, not one lucky batch of 1,000 samples. They also describe Wikipedia’s links within this roster—not how popular the characters are with readers, or how many friends they have in their stories.

02 / THE REPEAT GUESTS

Spider-Man keeps getting invited.

As the uniformly selected person, Spider-Man appears only 0.35% of the time: one chance in 286. As the selected friend, he appears 5.65% of the time—about 16 times as often. He belongs to many neighborhoods, so we keep meeting him through other people.

03 / COMPARED TO WHAT?

Scramble the universe.
The paradox stays.

Keep each character’s number of connections, but change who links to whom. If Marvel’s friend average survives, perhaps we never needed a special superhero explanation.

WHAT SURVIVES

The hubs explain a lot.

Against a random network with only the same size and edge count, Marvel looks extraordinary. Against a null that keeps every character’s degree, its friend average looks ordinary. We find no detectable excess beyond that degree-preserving baseline in this test.

The random graphs still have a small friendship paradox. Their friend average exceeds their eligible-person average by about 0.96 connections, compared with Marvel’s 14.20. Erasing the hubs shrinks the gap; it does not make all degrees equal.

The striking result is not that the effect disappears. It is how little it needs Marvel’s particular wiring.

This is evidence about one statistic and one null model. It does not show that every property of Marvel is random, or that communities and clustering do not matter.

05 / A SAMPLING TRAP

“Random friend” has two meanings.

The familiar formula says an edge endpoint has mean degree ⟨k²⟩ / ⟨k⟩ = ⟨k⟩ + variance / ⟨k⟩. In Marvel, that is 22.08. Our person-then-friend experiment gives 24.22. Both numbers are correct; they use different ways of choosing a friend.

A uniformly chosen edge endpoint weights each node by its degree. A friend of a uniformly chosen person weights it by the reciprocal degrees of the neighbors who might name it. The first average stays exactly fixed under degree-preserving swaps. The second can change with wiring, which is why its null distribution has a spread.

Check it on a four-person star

One person knows the other three; the three leaves each know only the center. The ordinary person mean is (3 + 1 + 1 + 1) / 4 = 1.5. A random edge endpoint has mean degree 2. A random person’s friend has mean (1 + 3 + 3 + 3) / 4 = 2.5.

For an eligible person i with degree kᵢ, the friend mean is the average of (1 / kᵢ) ∑ⱼ∼ᵢ kⱼ over eligible people. This is not generally the same as the edge-endpoint formula. The sampler uses the former throughout.

REPRODUCIBILITY

Same snapshot. A new question.

We use the course’s frozen 26 August 2026 snapshot: 303 roster nodes and 1,784 directed links. Adding the full roster first preserves the isolates. We collapse opposite arrows into 1,434 undirected pairs, retaining all 303 nodes. Degree counts unique neighbors.

For each pair, choose a person uniformly among nodes with positive degree and a neighbor uniformly from that person’s adjacency list. Draws are independent and made with replacement. The fraction includes ties. The all-node mean degree is 9.47; conditioning on the 286 eligible people raises the person baseline to 10.03. Named synthetic nodes keep the Marvel labels only for comparison; their edges are generated.

The degree null uses 100 fresh copies of Marvel and 14,340 successful simple-graph edge swaps per copy. Every labelled degree is checked afterward; connectivity is not constrained. Seeds start at 21109. The random null uses 100 G(n, m) graphs with 303 nodes and 1,434 edges, with seeds starting at 22109. The sampler’s shuffled and random options show the first graph in each ensemble.

We test the observed exact person-first friend mean against each ensemble with a two-sided equal-tail Monte Carlo test: p = min(1, 2 min(p₊, p₋)), where p₊ and p₋ use (1 + tail count) / 101. The degree-null p is 0.832; the random-null p is 0.0198, the smallest two-sided value resolvable with 100 realizations. Ten times the edge count is a practical shuffle length, not a proof of perfect mixing.

THE QUESTION

Are these similar characters—or similar words?

Last week we connected articles through exact repeated passages. This week we ignore word order and compare their weighted vocabularies. TF-IDF gives more weight to words that are distinctive across the corpus; cosine measures how closely two document vectors point in the same direction. A high score is lexical resemblance, not a verdict about character meaning.

Our test changes one thing: remove term types whose occurrences start with a capital letter more than half the time across the corpus. That is the course’s proposed shortcut for names. We rebuild the vectors and rank each page’s five closest neighbors again. No page is removed, and both versions keep numbers and English stopwords.

THE SURNAME TRAP

One word can build a whole resemblance.

Storm and Human Torch have an original cosine of 0.329. The shared coordinate storm supplies 86.9% of that score. The raw introductions explain why: one page is about Ororo Munroe, whose alias is Storm; the other introduces Jonathan Lowell Spencer “Johnny” Storm. The model cannot distinguish an alias from a surname.

Emma Frost’s first match is Jack Frost, at 0.463. Here frost supplies 96.1% of the cosine. Removing that term sends Jean Grey to first place. These two checks show a real failure mode: a rare shared name can outweigh most of a biography.

WHAT REPLACES IT?

From “storm” to “she” and “her”.

After filtering, Storm’s closest page is Jean Grey. That is a more recognizable X-Men connection. Yet her and she together supply 34.0% of the new cosine. For Storm’s second match, She-Hulk, they supply 58.2%. Words about mutants and relationships contribute too, but the score is not just about powers or shared stories.

Keeping English stopwords makes this visible. “She” and “her” are unevenly distributed across the roster, so they receive nonzero IDF weights. The tokenizer also splits She-Hulk, letting its name contribute to the same she coordinate. A seemingly semantic improvement partly reflects pronouns and tokenization. Removing stopwords would be a useful next experiment; it would be a second intervention, not evidence from this one.

THE COUNTEREXAMPLE

Some names are the story.

Wolverine’s first original match is his Ultimate Marvel counterpart, with cosine 0.599. After filtering, that counterpart leaves the top five; Black Panther comes first, narrowly ahead of Deadpool and Cyclops. Inspect the new first match: its largest contributions include particularly, argues and written, alongside claws. Editorial phrasing can rise when identity words disappear.

Jean Grey makes the same point another way. Her closest original page is the Phoenix Force, at 0.703; phoenix, jean and grey account for 87.4% of that score. Those are names, but they identify an actual host–entity relationship discussed in the articles. The filter treats meaningful shared history and coincidental surnames alike.

Removing names changes the neighborhood. It does not automatically improve it.
THE WHOLE ROSTER

Not a small adjustment.

The filter removes 34.1% of the vocabulary. Across all 303 pages, 223 first matches change (73.6%), and an average of only 1.39 of the original five neighbors survives in the new top five. The four examples were chosen to inspect surname collisions and meaningful names, not sampled to estimate how often each explanation occurs.

The original lists agree more often with direct Wikipedia links: 2.64 of five neighbors per page versus 1.39 after filtering. A link in either direction counts once. This is a descriptive comparison with the frozen hyperlink graph, not an accuracy benchmark: editors’ links reflect page scope, prominence and identity too. It does challenge the assumption that removing names is a universal repair.

The strongest conclusion is sensitivity. A preprocessing choice changes most first matches. To interpret a particular one, we need its contributing words and the article text—not just its rank.

REPRODUCIBILITY

Two vocabularies. One controlled comparison.

Exercise 6.11 uses the course’s 303 full Marvel Wikipedia pages from the frozen 26 August 2026 release. All roster pages remain in both analyses, including the hyperlink graph’s isolates. Page identities and URLs come from the Week 1 roster. One erroneous display label for Phoenix_Force is corrected to “Phoenix Force”; its ID and text are unchanged.

We use CountVectorizer with its default lowercasing and token pattern (?u)\b\w\w+\b: word tokens of at least two characters, splitting punctuation and hyphens. Numbers and stopwords stay. For each term, TF = count / retained document length and IDF = ln(303 / document frequency), with neither smoothing nor an added constant. Terms in every page therefore have zero weight. Vectors are L2-normalized before cosine; a word’s contribution is the product of its two normalized coordinates.

The filter counts case-sensitive [A-Za-z]+ occurrences across the raw corpus, lowercases their types, and marks those with an uppercase initial in strictly more than half their uses. We intersect that set with the vectorizer vocabulary and remove those columns from every document. The remaining document frequencies do not change; retained lengths and L2 norms are recalculated. This is not named entity recognition: sentence beginnings can remove ordinary words, lowercase names can survive, and non-ASCII names are not covered by the alphabetic heuristic.

All other pages are candidates for each top-five list. Self-matches are excluded and exact score ties break alphabetically by node ID. The downloadable JSON includes all top-five pairs from either view, each pair’s score and five largest contributions in both views, a deduplicated registry of first-occurrence raw excerpts, source hashes, versions and the removed-term audit. Passages are evidence about usage, not an exhaustive explanation of every occurrence.

Checks independently reconstruct counts and cosine rankings for the four inspected pages, verify every exported passage against its raw page, recompute summary claims, and exercise reversed pair orientation in the browser. The snapshot cannot establish character similarity as ground truth. A better model would need a stated target—shared powers, storyline, identity or editorial style—and an evaluation suited to that target.

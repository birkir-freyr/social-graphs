THE QUESTION

Can shared language find reused prose?

The first four weeks followed Wikipedia’s hyperlinks. This week every node becomes a document. We asked whether long, repeated sequences would uncover shared writing between character pages—and whether those matches actually meant what we thought they meant.

We counted consecutive token sequences, keeping their order. A pair of pages gets a link when their longest shared run reaches our chosen minimum. This is a new, undirected text network; it does not reuse the hyperlink edges from Week 1.

At eight tokens, 302 of 303 pages belong to one giant component, connected by 40,550 pairs: 88.6% of all possible pairs. One stock phrase—“in American comic books published by Marvel Comics”—occurs in 282 pages. Those pages alone supply 39,621 pairs, accounting for 97.7% of the eight-token links. The network mostly reveals an introductory formula, not distinctive shared writing.

THE LONGEST MATCH

164 tokens about the same alternate life.

Mayday Parker and the umbrella page for Spider-Woman share the longest run in the corpus. Both recount Claire and Benjy Parker’s childhood in Spider-Man: Life Story, Peter’s return to New York, and Morlun’s attack on the family. The entire extracted span is identical even before we lowercase it.

That is much more specific than the stock description of a superhero. Yet the surrounding text explains why the two articles meet: one discusses an alternate version resembling Mayday; the other discusses Claire as a Spider-Woman. We have found the same account in two overlapping article scopes, not evidence of two unrelated characters having the same biography.

Text inspection changes the claim. The longest shared passage is about Claire Parker, although one endpoint’s page is titled Mayday Parker.

THREE MORE CHECKS

Repeated words, different reasons.

Cyclops ↔ Jean Grey: 99 tokens. Both pages describe Cyclops leaving his family to lead X-Factor, Claremont’s objection, and the book’s writers. The sequence is identical after normalization; a paragraph break differs. This is shared editorial history, not just character abilities.

Spitfire ↔ Union Jack / Joseph Chapman: 123 tokens. This initially looks like a spectacular prose match. Open it and you find collected editions: creators, issue numbers, page counts, dates and ISBNs. Related characters share a bibliography. Keeping numbers in the tokenizer makes this match more demanding, but does not turn it into a narrative.

Spider-Woman ↔ Jessica Drew: 128 tokens. Both reproduce the same Stan Lee quotation about protecting the Spider-Woman name. Differences in quote marks and punctuation disappear under tokenization. A quotation can legitimately recur in two articles, so calling every long match “copying” would overstate the evidence.

The graph found repeated text. Reading it tells us what kind.
WHAT SURPRISED US

A network of pages is also a network of writing.

With a 30-token minimum, only 38 pages remain connected by any shared passage, spread across 14 small groups. The biggest has six pages. At eight tokens, nearly everyone belongs to one group. The surprise is how much apparent language similarity is supplied by a standard introduction—and how diverse the surviving longer matches are.

This matters if we later compare characters or communities using word counts. Repeated passages are not independent descriptions: they can amplify a topic simply because the same writing occurs in several places. We have not estimated how much of the entire corpus is duplicated, and one longest run per pair is not a measure of total overlap. It is enough to show why the raw text needs to sit beside the statistic.

REPRODUCIBILITY

Every pair, with the passage attached.

We use the course’s 303 full Marvel Wikipedia articles from the frozen 26 August 2026 release, joined by node_id to the Week 1 roster. The corpus supplies 725,178 tokens under this post’s tokenizer. All 45,753 unordered pairs are in scope; every page remains in the analysis even when it has no match.

The token pattern is \w+(?:[-'’]\w+)*. We lowercase the resulting pieces, retain numbers, stopwords, internal apostrophes and hyphens, and treat other punctuation and whitespace as boundaries. Straight and curly apostrophes are not made equivalent. These counts deliberately differ from analyses that remove numbers or split hyphenated words.

An inverted index of all eight-token sequences finds every possible cross-page starting match. We skip a start only if both previous tokens also match, because the same alignment has an earlier beginning. We extend the remaining starts until the sequences diverge and keep the longest run for each pair. Repeated occurrences are considered; ties prefer the earliest source and target token offsets. No phrases cross document boundaries. This is exact sequence matching, not semantic similarity or a copying classifier.

The CSV contains every pair with a run of eight or more tokens. The browser JSON contains every pair with a run of at least 20, along with its two raw source spans and character offsets. The graph uses those pairs once each, regardless of how many other passages match. Static and interactive views use a seeded layout of the 20-token graph, with its connected groups arranged in a grid; colors identify those groups. Position and color are not additional linguistic measurements.

We inspected the longest matches and their surrounding text, checked every exported passage against the frozen source, and tested the search against an independent longest-common-substring calculation on small sequences with repeated patterns. The cutoff has no calibrated false-positive rate. Without revision history, this snapshot cannot tell us which page borrowed from which, whether both used a third source, or what editors intended.

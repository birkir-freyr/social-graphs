THE QUESTION

The answer can be on the page and still be hard to find.

A biography can contain a perfect answer to a narrow query while spending most of its words on other things. If we average the whole page into one vector, a short origin story has to share its representation with decades of plots, publication history and adaptations.

We compare four retrieval rules on the same corpus: a full-page TF-IDF keyword baseline; a pretrained encoder’s page mean; the page’s best 180-word passage; and its best 60-word passage. Every method ranks the same 303 pages for the same eight saved queries.

There are two changes to distinguish. Page mean → best passage changes how evidence is pooled. Then 180 → 60 words changes window size while maximum pooling stays fixed. Calling the entire comparison a chunk-size effect would mix those choices together.

WHEN PASSAGES HELP

A local passage brings the intended page forward.

For “a surgeon who learned sorcery after a car accident,” TF-IDF puts Doctor Strange third, behind Coldblood and Doctor Nemesis. Coldblood’s page describes cybernetic surgery; Human Torch’s page mentions a surgeon and a car accident involving his parents. Those are genuine word matches that satisfy only part of the request.

The page-mean encoder ranks Strange fifth. Both passage methods put him first. The 180-word passage describes his surgical career, the crash and damaged hands, then begins his search for the Ancient One. The 60-word result focuses even more narrowly on the crash and lost surgical ability. That short excerpt alone does not establish the turn to magic: expand the source context to check the remaining constraint.

Black Widow shows a similar benefit from changing pooling. For “a Russian spy who became an Avenger,” Natasha’s page moves from 20th under page means to third under 180-word max. But shortening to 60 words moves her back to fifth. TF-IDF puts her first. The dense encoder has not universally beaten keyword search, and a smaller window has not universally improved the result.

WHEN THE TARGET MOVES

Spider-Man comes back. The query stays ambiguous.

For “a young hero bitten by a radioactive spider,” the intended Spider-Man page ranks fourth with page means, sixth with 180-word passages, and first with 60-word passages. That looks like a striking recovery if we inspect only the target-rank plot.

Read the 180-word winner, John Jameson. His page describes a What If? version in which he receives the radioactive-spider bite and fights crime as Spider-Jameson. This is a plausible literal match to an underspecified query. Spider-Girl also contains alternate spider-origin stories. Moving Peter Parker higher does not prove every alternative was wrong.

The keyword baseline has a clearer collision: it puts Radioactive Man first. A rare shared word can dominate lexical search. Passage retrieval changes the failure mode, but it still needs a question precise enough to judge.

THE PAGE IS NOT THE CLAIM

Finding Venom does not settle what the passage says.

“A living alien suit that bonds with a host” puts Venom 17th with page means and first with either passage size. Yet the top 180-word passage describes the Ultimate version and explicitly says the symbiote is “not extraterrestrial.” The model finds suit-and-host language while the passage contradicts the alien constraint.

The 60-word winner falls inside that same alternative-origin discussion and omits the earlier negation. Its page rank is also 1. Expanding the surrounding source text reveals what the short result leaves out. Page retrieval and evidence for a specific statement are different checks.

A better rank is useful. The passage still has to answer the question.
THE CORPUS HAS AN EDGE

No window can retrieve a page we do not have.

The saved blind-lawyer query was intended to find Daredevil. That page is absent from this 303-page roster. We discovered the gap when checking the results and retained it as a coverage example. Every method still returns five pages; none of those rankings demonstrates that the intended answer exists in the corpus.

Eight assistant-written queries are enough to inspect behaviors, not enough to establish a general winner. Their intended targets were recorded before the original local search run; this article’s question and highlighted examples were selected after those results were seen. Related incarnations and umbrella pages complicate binary relevance labels. We report all eight queries and avoid turning target rank into an accuracy claim.

REPRODUCIBILITY

Change the search unit. Keep the evidence.

Exercise 7.13 explicitly suggests comparing semantic search with keyword search, then reading the returned pages. We use all 303 raw Marvel Wikipedia pages from the frozen 26 August 2026 course snapshot. The roster defines the candidate set. Queries and intended page IDs are stored with the analysis.

The sparse baseline follows Week 6’s TF-IDF definition: CountVectorizer lowercases tokens of at least two word characters, retaining numbers and English stopwords. IDF is ln(303/DF), with no smoothing or added constant. Corpus vectors and queries are L2-normalized using corpus IDF. Raw versus relative term frequency gives identical cosine after normalization. Vocabulary and IDF are fitted on the 303 pages; query text does not change them.

All dense views use sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2, revision 1110a243fdf4706b3f48f1d95db1a4f5529b4d41, with 384-dimensional outputs and a 256-wordpiece input limit. Raw text is cut into contiguous windows of at most 180 or 60 whitespace words, shortened if necessary to at most 254 wordpieces before two special tokens. There are 4,214 larger passages and 12,187 smaller passages; all non-whitespace source text is covered. Passage boundaries can cut sentences and different window sizes create different boundaries.

The page mean averages the L2-normalized 180-word passage vectors equally, then normalizes the mean. It represents the full page rather than silently truncating it to the opening. Max-passage retrieval scores every passage and assigns each page its highest query cosine. Longer pages have more opportunities to produce a high maximum. The 180→60 comparison holds the model and max-pooling rule fixed; page mean→max passage also changes pooling.

Every method ranks all 303 pages. Exact score ties break alphabetically by node ID; tied passages use the earliest source offset. The browser shows the first five and allows inspection of the intended page even when it ranks lower. TF-IDF and page-mean source excerpts are selected for inspection; their ranking scores still use the full page. In the two max-passage views, the shown original passage defines the page score. Expanding context adds up to 800 preceding and 1,200 following source characters without reranking.

The public package bundles the raw corpus, exact query/document/passage vectors, source offsets, all 9,696 ranking rows and offline build scripts. Optional encoding code reruns the pinned pretrained model, downloading its weights on the first run. Tests reconstruct lexical scores, dense scores, maxima, rankings, source spans and reported target ranks. Source-reading notes are assistant assessments, not independent human validation. No answer generator is used, so the results isolate retrieval behavior.

Our conclusion is conditional: changing the unit and pooling rule changes which pages and passages surface. Smaller windows rescue some intended targets, hurt others and can hide qualifying context. These eight exploratory queries do not establish a universally best chunk size or search method.
